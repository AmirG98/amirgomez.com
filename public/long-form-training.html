01

Three principles before you record or write

1 · Leaving is the default. Nobody owes your content their attention. Every second, the viewer decides again whether to stay. Retention is not luck or charisma: it is a structure that makes staying easier than leaving.

2 · Retention is a managed promise. Viewers stay as long as they believe what is coming is worth more than what they have already seen. Your job is to open a clear promise early, pay it in installments, and always owe them one more.

3 · Conversion is a byproduct of trust. In long-form you do not convert by interrupting: you convert because after 15 minutes of real help, the next step with you is the logical continuation. First you earn attention, then you earn permission to offer.

People do not leave because the content is long. They leave because they stopped believing the ending is worth it.

Grounding: Nielsen Norman Group measured that most abandonment happens in the first 10 seconds — and that past the half-minute mark, the odds of staying rise dramatically [1]. YouTube officially recommends treating the first 15 seconds as the critical retention zone [2].

03

The method: 7 steps to build the piece

1
First 30 seconds · attacks Leak 1

The hook: outcome + mistake + open loop

Before greeting anyone, before introducing yourself: name the exact outcome the viewer wants, the mistake most people make chasing it, and one specific promise that only pays off by watching the whole piece. No "in this video we’re going to cover...".

Example"You’re going to build your first campaign that sells on its own. 90% of people start with the ad — and that’s why they burn money. The right order has 5 steps, and the fifth is the one almost nobody does: I’ll show it at the end with a real case."
2
Minute 1 · the map

Show the destination and the path in 30 seconds

Show the finished end result (the campaign running, the dish plated, the furniture assembled) and the map of steps you will walk together. People decide to stay when they see that (a) the destination is real and (b) the path is finite. A visual "index" of 5–7 steps lowers anxiety and lifts retention.

3
The body · value stepping stones

Every step = a complete win on its own

Structure the content so every step leaves something usable even if the viewer left right there. Name the step, show it (don’t narrate it: do it on screen), and close it with the visible "partial result". The feeling of constant progress is the best retainer there is.

Practical ruleIf a step runs longer than 2–3 minutes with nothing changing on screen and no mini-win, split it in two.
4
Between steps · attacks Leak 2

Re-hooks: close one door, open the next

The middle is where long-form dies. When each step ends, do two things in one sentence: recap what they gained + preview the cost of leaving: "You now have X. But X without the next step breaks in two weeks — fixing it takes 5 minutes now or 5 hours later."

ToolPlant 2–3 loops that only close at the end: "the step-5 mistake", "the exact number I use", "the before/after of the real case".
5
Every 60–90 seconds

Change something: the brain stays where there is motion

Alternate framing, pace, visual device, tone. A chart, a zoom into detail, a 20-second story, a "wait — this matters". You don’t need expensive production: you need deliberate variation. Audiovisual monotony kills pieces with excellent content.

6
The bridge · attacks Leak 3

From teaching to offering without breaking trust

The classic mistake: cutting the teaching to start "the sell". The right bridge shows the natural limit of do-it-yourself: "You can do all of this on your own, and with this video you will. Now, if you want X faster / at more scale / without the trial and error, that’s exactly what we do with clients." The offer appears as the next rung of the same ladder, not a change of subject.

7
The close

One single CTA, said as an instruction

One concrete, imperative CTA, repeated at most twice (softly at the mid-piece value peak; directly at the end). Say what to do, what happens next and how long it takes: "Book the 15-minute call — you leave with your plan, whether you apply it with us or on your own." And close by closing the loops you opened in the hook: the ending should feel like a debt paid.

04

Toolbox: retention

→Open loops

Promise something specific for later and name it now and then. The brain hates unfinished stories.

→Specificity

"I raised CTR 2.3x in 11 days" retains; "improve your results" repels. Concrete numbers are hooks.

→Show > tell

Screen, hands, real process. Every minute of talking head without visual support is a minute at risk.

→Callbacks

Return to an earlier joke, phrase or example. It rewards those who stayed and creates a shared-journey feeling.

→"Most people fail here"

Pointing at the exact spot where others go wrong re-hooks: nobody wants to be most people.

→Edit pace

Cut silences and filler. In step-by-step, the standard is never letting 5 seconds pass without new information.

05

Toolbox: conversion

→CTA at the peak, not the door

The moment of highest perceived value (right after the biggest win) is where a soft CTA performs best.

→Stacked proof

Before the final CTA: a case, a number, a client quote. 20 seconds of proof beat 2 minutes of arguments.

→De-risk the step

The CTA doesn’t sell the product: it sells the next step (a call, a demo, a template). Small, clear, frictionless.

→Honest urgency

If there is a real reason to act now (spots, date, price), say it. If there isn’t, don’t invent one: it burns the trust you built.

→The "even if you never buy"

"Apply it on your own and it will work" — paradoxically, letting go of the sell is what sells most in long-form.

→One funnel per piece

Each long piece points to ONE destination (a landing, a lead magnet, a call). Two different CTAs = half the conversion on each.

Golden rule: the healthy ratio in a step-by-step piece is 90% teaching / 10% offering — and the 10% works precisely because the 90% was genuinely generous.

The CTA in depth: anatomy, types, timing and wording

Anatomy of a CTA that works. Four parts, always: imperative verb + concrete object + what happens next + how long it takes. "Book your 15-minute call — you leave with your plan built" has all four. "Contact us for more information" has none.

The 3 CTA types — and when to use each. Not every audience is ready for the same ask; long-form usually needs two of these three:

  • Direct (booking, purchase, application): for the hot viewer who reached the end. Just one, at the close, with the step-6 bridge already built.
  • Transitional (template, checklist, lead magnet): the "easy yes" for whoever is not ready yet — StoryBrand formalizes it as the direct + transitional CTA pair every brand needs [6]. You capture the email and continue the conversation.
  • Continuity (watch the next piece, subscribe): keeps the pure learner inside your ecosystem. On YouTube, it is also the CTA that feeds the algorithm.

Where to place it: timing is half the conversion. Wistia’s classic study across thousands of videos with CTAs found that mid-roll CTAs convert best (≈17%), above post-roll (≈11%) and far above pre-roll (≈3%) [3]. The right reading: the best moment is right after a value peak, when trust is highest — not when the piece is already saying goodbye and attention is gone. In practice: one soft mention after the method’s first big win, and the direct CTA at the close.

How many: one destination, few mentions. Every extra option competes with your conversion — that is Unbounce’s 1:1 attention ratio principle: a page (or piece) with one goal should have one clickable destination [5]. In video: one single place to send people; name it at most 2–3 times. In long articles: the same CTA repeated per section counts as one.

Wording that moves the needle (all of this is tested, not opinion):

  • First person. "I want my plan" beats "Get your plan" — Michael Aagaard’s classic test measured +90% CTR by switching "your" to "my" on the button [7].
  • Reader context. HubSpot, across 330,000 CTAs: CTAs personalized to who is looking convert 202% better than generic ones [4]. In long-form this means speaking to the exact moment: "Made it this far? Then this is for you."
  • Specificity of the after. Saying what happens post-click (who writes to you, what you receive, when) lowers friction more than any "it’s free!".
  • Reversed risk. "You leave with your plan, whether you apply it with us or on your own" — explicit generosity triggers reciprocity, Cialdini’s first principle [8].
Why 90/10 works (the psychology underneath): open loops retain through the Zeigarnik effect — the unfinished occupies the mind until it closes [9]; curiosity fires when there is a concrete information gap between what I know and what I want to know (Loewenstein) [10]; and an hour of genuine help creates the reciprocity debt the final CTA collects (Cialdini) [8]. The whole method is these three mechanisms placed in order.
07

The 6 mistakes we see most

  • Introducing yourself first. Nobody wants your bio before knowing what they gain. Credibility is demonstrated by teaching, not announced.
  • Promising in generic. "Marketing tips" retains no one. One outcome, one timeframe, one named method.
  • Saving "the good part" for the end without saying so. If viewers don’t know something better is coming, they don’t stay to find out. The loop must be opened explicitly.
  • Steps that are theory. A step without visible action is a podcast chapter. In step-by-step, every block ends with something done.
  • The handbrake pitch. Selling while apologizing ("well, if you’re interested, I don’t know, maybe...") signals even you don’t believe in your offer. The step-6 bridge exists for this.
  • Publishing without reading the curve. The first piece is a hypothesis. Without reading where people drop, the second piece repeats the same mistakes.

09

After publishing: read the curve and iterate

The 4 readings that matter (YouTube Studio, your VSL player’s analytics, or whatever tool you use):

  • Retention at 30s. If you lose more than 30–40% there, the problem is the hook (step 1) — don’t touch anything else yet.
  • The slope of the middle. Smooth, constant decline is normal; sharp "cliffs" mark the exact minute of a boring step or a missing re-hook.
  • The cliff right before the CTA. If people leave just before the offer, the bridge (step 6) is announcing "the sell starts now". Soften it or move it.
  • CTA conversion (clicks or bookings / viewers who reached the CTA). Measure it over those who arrived, not the total: they are different decisions.

Iterate one variable per piece: hook first, then middle, then bridge. In 3–4 pieces the curve changes shape — and conversions with it.

Publishing is step one of content. Reading the curve is where the craft is learned.
